# Score every fight, skip whole-game deep RL

Cameo cannot build a deep-RL bot that learns "as many parameters as possible" over the whole game on one PC. The cheapest documented full-game RTS learner needed about 60,000 games just to beat StarCraft II's *Easy* AI, and that was on one map, one matchup and 165 scripted macro actions. Cameo's entire yearly budget is 36,000–55,000 matches, spread over ~325 faction pairings, on a ruleset that changes every few weeks. Cameo can still build a much smarter and more varied bot from the "log everything, score every engagement" idea, if the learning is split by how much signal each kind of parameter gets. **Thousands of coefficients can be *measured* directly from logs**: combat strengths, response times, enemy timing priors. **A few tens of thousands of weights fit in a small Supreme Commander 2-style engagement network**, because every match yields dozens of labelled fights. **Only about 8–24 knobs at a time can be *tuned* against win rate**, because a 5-point win-rate difference takes 783–3,130 matches to detect. Discrete choices (personality, opening, attack plan) sit on hierarchical Thompson bandits that pool evidence across factions. The project's existing rulings line up with this architecture rather than holding it back: bounded multipliers on defaults, weights frozen at match start, training on dev builds only, and fog honesty. Variety comes from sampling among proven-good options and varying *where and when* pressure lands, while a deterministic combat-sim veto stops any random draw from producing the blunders players remember. Rebalance robustness comes from learning only *residuals* over stat-derived priors that the balance pipeline recomputes for free. The earlier "deep RL: not now" verdict stands for the whole-game policy. It should be refined to "yes, a small learned network for engagement decisions, once logging and the combat evaluator exist".

## Whole-game deep RL misses the budget by two to three orders of magnitude

Every RTS system that learned a whole-game policy did so with industrial compute, a large human-replay prior, or a drastically reduced problem. **AlphaStar ran 12 league agents, each on 32 TPUv3 for 44 days with 16,000 concurrent StarCraft II games**, after supervised pretraining on **971,000 human replays** ([Vinyals et al., Nature 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). OpenAI Five used **~159M parameters, 770±50 PFlops/s-days and ~45,000 years of self-play** ([Berner et al., arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06680); [OpenAI blog](https://openai.com/index/openai-five-defeats-dota-2-world-champions/)). Tencent's reduced-scale reimplementation, TStarBot-X, had 144 V100s and 13,440 CPU cores. It found that "a faithful reimplementation of AlphaStar's methods can not succeed" at that scale, and its main agent collapsed into a single rush strategy within days ([Han et al., arXiv 2011.13729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13729)).

The affordable end of the literature is the informative part. **Gym-μRTS reached state of the art in ~60 hours on one GPU, but on a single 16×16 map** ([Huang et al., arXiv 2105.13807](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.13807)). **RAISocketAI**, the first deep-RL bot to win the microRTS competition, used **70 GPU-days and 1.5 billion steps** and still scored only **34% on the largest 64×64 map** ([Goodfriend, arXiv 2402.08112](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08112)). TStarBot1 learned only *which of 165 hand-scripted macros to run*. It needed roughly **0.06M games to beat Easy, 0.5M to beat Hard and 7M to beat the cheating level-10 AI** on one map in a Zerg mirror ([Sun et al., arXiv 1809.07193](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.07193)). Learning the full game from win/loss alone failed outright in SC2LE: agents "cannot learn to win against the easiest built-in AI on the full game" ([Vinyals et al., arXiv 1708.04782](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04782)).

The table puts Cameo's budget next to these data points. The Cameo rows are arithmetic on the stated budget, not sourced figures.

| System | What it learned | Data / compute | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| AlphaStar Final | Whole SC2 game, 3 races | 12 agents × 32 TPUv3 × 44 days; 971k replays | Grandmaster, 99.8th percentile |
| TStarBot1 | Choice among 165 scripted macros, 1 map, 1 matchup | ~60k games to beat Easy; 7M for level-10 | 90%+ vs levels 1–9 |
| RAISocketAI | Whole microRTS game | 70 GPU-days, 1.5B steps | 72% overall; 34% on 64×64 |
| Supreme Commander 2 | Fight/flee + target type per platoon, 34→98→15 MLP | "about an hour of training" per network | Shipped 2010 |
| **Cameo, one year** | — | **36k–55k matches** (100–150/day) | — |
| **Cameo, one balance increment** | — | **~700–3,000 matches** (1–3 weeks) | — |

Three structural facts make the gap worse than the raw counts suggest. First, **coverage multiplies cost**. AlphaStar trained a separate network per race and showed no cross-race transfer ([Nature 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). Honor of Kings documents how pairing combinations explode from 4.9M for 17 heroes to 213 billion for 40 ([Ye et al., arXiv 2011.12692](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12692)). Cameo's ~25 factions give ~325 unordered pairings. Second, **the target moves**. OpenAI Five survived patches only through 20+ "surgeries" and gradual annealing. A clean rerun on the final version needed only ~20% of the compute and **beat the continually-patched agent more than 98% of the time** ([arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06680)). Training through constant change costs skill. Third, **there is no human prior**. Imitation at useful scale needs 10⁵–10⁶ replays ([Lin et al., STARDATA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02139)), and a mod has nothing near that.

The direct answer to "deep RL for as many parameters as possible" is therefore no for the whole-game policy, and the margin is two to three orders of magnitude, not a tuning problem. The useful reframing comes from Gym-μRTS: action-space design "is more important to the performance than the sheer number of the model's trainable parameters" ([arXiv 2105.13807](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.13807)). What limits Cameo is the independent signal available per parameter, not the parameter count. A fight is a sample, and a match is only one sample of win/loss. Spend learnable parameters where signal is dense.

## Four tiers spend parameters where the signal is dense

The number of parameters that can be learned depends on how each one is estimated. A coefficient *measured* directly from logged events (how much damage a delivery class does to an armour class in practice) needs no outcome experiment. A weight trained from per-engagement scores gets dozens of samples per match. A knob *tuned against win rate* gets one noisy bit per match, so only a handful can move at once. The ranked architecture below follows that gradient. It maps onto the three routes the project already planned and adds one, the engagement network.

| Rank | Tier (route) | What it learns | Realistic parameter count | Data needed | Wall-clock at 100–150/day | Compute |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Measured** (route 1) | Lanchester strength residuals per delivery×armour cell, attrition exponent, static-defence state strengths, response-time and enemy-timing priors per enemy faction | **~10³** (e.g. a ~260-cell grid plus defence states plus timing priors) | ~500 logged battles per fit ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)) | **1–5 days** for a first fit | Seconds of CPU (closed-form MLE) |
| 2 | **Veto/gate** (no learning, consumes tier 1) | Nothing new; it reads the evaluator | 0 | — | Ships with tier 1 | Microseconds per query |
| 3 | **Chosen** (route 3, bandits + archive) | Personality × opening × attack-plan cell posteriors per matchup, hierarchically pooled | **~10²–10³ posteriors**, few effective degrees of freedom | Tens of matches per arm-cell *after* pooling | Weeks to converge per matchup family | Negligible |
| 4 | **Tuned** (route 2, SPSA / BO) | Bounded multipliers on defaults | **~8 per faction; 8–24 in flight at once** | 2,000 paired iterations ≈ 4,000 matches per tune | **~27 days** per 8-knob tune | Negligible |
| 5 | **Engagement network** (new) | Engage/flank/siege-first/retreat/target utilities for a squad meeting resistance | **~5k weights per network, ~15–20k total** for 3–4 networks | ~50k–100k logged engagements with exploration | **1–3 weeks** of logging | Minutes to an hour of CPU per training run |
| 6 | Whole-game deep RL | Everything | 10⁶–10⁸ | 10⁵–10⁷ games | Years to decades | GPU-weeks to GPU-years |

Tier 1 is where "log everything" pays off fastest. Stanescu, Barriga and Buro modelled a heterogeneous army as average strength λ = Σ DPS×HP and fitted per-type strengths by maximum likelihood from about **500 logged battles**. They used a regulariser Σ(λ−default)² that pulls each strength toward its stats-derived default, which is the formal version of Cameo's "bounded multiplier on default" ruling. Their predictor reached **89.8% winner accuracy from just 10 training battles and 93.2% from 500**. Inside UAlbertaBot, learned strengths lifted the attack/retreat win rate from **63.9% (defaults) to 69.7%**, beating full simulation at 60.8% ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)). The authors note the fit is cheap enough to run "once after several games or even at the end of a game". Closed-form attrition models predict as well as simulators (**~93.5–94.1% vs SparCraft's 84.7–91.8%**) and run **19–145× faster** ([Uriarte & Ontañón, arXiv 1605.05305](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.05305)). Cameo's DPS input is the versus-resolved weapon against the actual armour mix, which the balance tooling already computes. Static defences and garrisons need state-dependent strength entries: the BW bunker needed **five separate strength values** for 0–4 marines inside ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)).

Tier 4 is bounded by statistics, not compute. Stockfish's SPSA tunes consume **30,000–120,000 games** each for 7–35 variables ([Kiiski on TalkChess](https://www.talkchess.com/forum3/viewtopic.php?t=40662)). At Cameo's rate that is 200–800 days. Detecting a 5-point win-rate change against a fixed 50% reference needs about **783 matches at 80% power**. Comparing two unpaired variants needs **~1,565 per arm, ~21 days in total**. These are normal-approximation figures from the optimisation notes, built on the standard sample-size scaling ([Wikipedia: sample size determination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size_determination)). Two design choices claw this back. Paired matches with common random numbers (same map, spawn, seed and opponent for the +c and −c arms) scale the requirement by (1−ρ). Fishtest plays games in pairs for exactly this reason ([Fishtest FAQ](https://official-stockfish.github.io/docs/fishtest-wiki/Fishtest-FAQ.html)). A continuous match score (win plus an army- or economy-value margin) can cut matches per arm by roughly 9× *if* the margin carries the same signal with less noise, which Cameo must measure. Two more limits apply. CMA-ES is typically applied in 3–100 dimensions and needs O(n²) generations to learn its covariance ([Hansen, CMA-ES](http://www.cmap.polytechnique.fr/~nikolaus.hansen/cmaesintro.html)). Standard Gaussian-process BO degrades past ~15–20 dimensions ([BoTorch discussion #1120](https://github.com/meta-pytorch/botorch/discussions/1120)). Both point at the same ~8-knob ceiling. With 25 factions, 150 matches/day is only ~6 matches per faction per day. The practical shape is therefore one family-level knob vector tuned at a time, with per-faction deltas shrunk toward it (see the faction section). Running 25 tunes in parallel is not feasible.

### The Supreme Commander 2 pattern is the one place a neural network earns its keep

Supreme Commander 2 shipped four MLPs (land, naval, bomber, fighter), each with **34 inputs, 98 hidden nodes and 15 outputs**, about **4,900 weights** (my arithmetic). A scripted platoon state machine queried its network "when the platoon encountered enemy resistance". The inputs were 17 friend/enemy ratios and their reciprocals: unit count, health, DPS, speed, resource value, shields, and short/medium/long-range DPS, gathered in a radius around the platoon. The outputs were utilities for actions such as "attack the weakest enemy … attack the highest value enemy … attack from range", and the platoon fled "if none of the network's outputs were above 0.5". It trained by having AI platoons take *random* actions in fast-forward skirmishes, scored by before/after strength ratios. That took "about an hour of training to reach a shippable level of performance". A query never took more than **0.03 ms** in an eight-player match ([Robbins, Game AI Pro ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter30_Using_Neural_Networks_to_Control_Agent_Threat_Response.pdf)).

This pattern fits Cameo almost exactly. Bots run host-only and act only through orders, so a fixed-weight MLP inside a squad or attack module only changes which orders are issued, and lockstep sync covers orders, not the bot's internal arithmetic (my inference from the stated architecture). A Cameo version would have roughly 40–60 inputs. These would be fog-honest ratios of tier-1 strength split by range band, artillery-range advantage, static-defence strength in the target region, AA and detection coverage, and reinforcement distance. It would have about 64 hidden nodes and about 6–8 outputs: direct assault, flank via an alternate approach, siege-first (hold until long-range units engage the defences), harass the weakest target, target-class priority, and retreat. That is again about 4–5k weights per network. Three or four networks (ground attack, air, defence response, possibly naval) total about 15–20k weights. That is the honest answer to "as many parameters as possible": roughly 10⁴ learned weights in a place where each match supplies dozens of labelled decisions, not 10⁷ weights in a place where each match supplies one bit.

Two adaptations matter. First, data from the scripted bot alone only shows the outcomes of the bot's own habits. SC2 got counterfactual coverage by taking random actions during training. Cameo's dev builds should therefore pick a non-default but veto-approved action in a small fraction of engagements and log the probability of that choice. This makes the training contextual-bandit learning from logged exploration (supervised regression of the engagement score on context and action), not full RL. It also allows off-policy checks before shipping. This is my design inference; the exploration-plus-fitness idea is SC2's. Second, the SC2 author's warnings are the cost of admission. A neural network is "a black box … You can't just go in, set a breakpoint". Changing "its inputs, the interpretation of its outputs, the fitness function, and the number of hidden nodes" forces retraining from scratch. A fitness bug shipped an AI that overwhelmed players with massive armies "but, as soon as it saw an ACU, it would turn tail and run", because the commander's death explosion poisoned the score ([Game AI Pro ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter30_Using_Neural_Networks_to_Control_Agent_Threat_Response.pdf)). The mitigations are to log every query's inputs and outputs, keep the scripted logic as the fallback, put the network behind an increment switch, and keep it last in the build order. The network is only as good as the tier-1 evaluator and the engagement score it learns from.

### Learning between matches, adapting within matches, without breaking the freeze rule

The request asks for bots that "learn in-match and between matches". The ruling that learned values are read frozen at match start allows both, provided the two are kept apart. *Between* matches, the weights, priors, posteriors and knobs update. *Within* a match, nothing learned changes. Instead, a fixed, bounded adaptation rule (whose gains are among the tuned knobs) reacts to evidence from the current game. Examples: a running engagement score against the current enemy composition that shifts an aggression bias within ±bounds; a Bayesian update of the enemy's plan class from scouting; a per-match Lanchester strength correction for units the enemy has upgraded. SC2's networks did the same with an aggression bias added to fixed outputs to create personalities without retraining ([Game AI Pro ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter30_Using_Neural_Networks_to_Control_Agent_Threat_Response.pdf)). The adaptation state resets each match, so reproducibility and the A/B workflow survive. Stanescu's per-opponent strength fitting, cheap enough to run "at the end of a game", is the template for turning that in-match evidence into between-match updates ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)).

## Log engagements as records, not as shots

The literature agrees on what to log, but not every frame. Research datasets log at multiple resolutions: economy every 25 frames, attacks every frame, positions every 100 frames, or every 7 frames during combat. Logging every frame "can grow too much to be processed efficiently" ([Uriarte & Ontañón, arXiv 1605.05305](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.05305)). The useful unit of analysis is the closed **engagement record** CR = ⟨t₀, t_f, R, A₀, B₀, A_f, B_f, K, P⟩: start and end time, end reason, both armies at start and end, kill sequence, and passive units that never fought. End reasons are army destroyed, *peace* (no attacks for 6 s), reinforcement, or game end ([Uriarte & Ontañón](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.05305)). Ending on reinforcement fragments fights badly: **99,598 of 140,797 combats (71%)** ended that way in their dataset. Whole-engagement scoring should therefore use STARDATA-style space-time clustering. That recipe uses a unit ball of **20 s and 200 walktiles**, at least **3 deaths**, **±6 s padding**, and merges at Jaccard > 0.6 ([Lin et al., STARDATA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02139)). Reinforcement arrivals are logged as sub-events. Stanescu's in-bot logger also drops non-battles where total damage was tiny and both sides lost less than 20% of army HP, and still got **5–30 battles per game** ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)). These constants have to be converted to OpenRA ticks and cells. No OpenRA-specific segmentation literature exists, so the conversion is a design choice to validate against replays.

The table lists what to add to Cameo's existing per-match records, ~150-tick snapshots, mission cards, placements and arsenal ledger. Entries without a citation are proposed operationalisations; the research found no peer-reviewed quantitative definitions for flanking, siege order or "attacking into defences".

| Record (when emitted) | Fields | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| **Engagement record** (on close) | CR tuple above; units and value per side per armour/delivery class; damage by source class and by target class; static defences and garrisons involved with their state; **evaluator-predicted remainder at t₀** vs actual; end reason | Tier-1 fit, engagement score, network targets |
| **Decision record** (when a squad commits, retreats or is vetoed) | Mission id, target region, chosen action and its sampling probability, network inputs/outputs if queried, veto reason if vetoed, personality and arm ids | Credit assignment, off-policy evaluation, veto diagnostics |
| **Approach geometry** (at first shot of an engagement) | Bearing of each own attacker around the enemy centroid (circular variance); angle between approach vectors of separate groups (flank if > ~60°); fraction of own army in weapon range of at least one enemy (concave / "surface area", the inverse of passive units) | Flank and concave metrics |
| **Siege order** (within engagements that include static defence) | Tick of first damage to each defence and its source range class vs the defence's range; own HP lost to defences before short-range units entered range | Artillery-first vs direct-assault scoring |
| **Base-attack response** (on first damage to an owned structure or harvester) | First-damage tick; tick the bot first *noticed* (Steamhammer-style stamp); tick the first defender entered range; value lost before response; whether the defence held the home reserve | Response-time metric and defence net |
| **Enemy plan stamps** (first observation, fog-honest) | First-seen ticks of enemy combat units, tech tiers, air, stealth, detection, expansions; coarse plan label (rush / fast expand / tech / turtle) | Plan recognition, per-enemy-faction priors ([Steamhammer game records](http://satirist.org/ai/starcraft/blog/archives/909-Steamhammer-3.0-game-record-format.html)) |
| **Snapshot additions** (existing ~150-tick cadence, burst while an engagement is open) | Army centroid path distance to nearest enemy-held region (normalised by map size); production and harvester idle time; value collected / spent / killed / lost by category, mirroring SC2's ScoreDetails schema ([score.proto](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Blizzard/s2client-proto/master/s2clientprotocol/score.proto)) | Positioning metric, match-level margin |
| **Match record** (end) | Factions, map, spawns, seed, build hash, ruleset fingerprint, personality/opening/knob vector, result, margin score | Bandits, SPSA/BO, drift detection |

Two engineering rules come from the sources. JSONL for low-rate event and decision records, with batched writes off the game thread and verbose logging as the exception, follows the AWS games guidance ([AWS Games Industry Lens](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/games-industry-lens/gameperf06-bp03.html)). Delta encoding plus Zstandard, which gave STARDATA **38× compression**, is worth adding only if Cameo starts recording per-unit position traces ([STARDATA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02139)).

One policy question needs a maintainer ruling rather than a default. Logs written on the host can contain omniscient information (both armies' true state), and AlphaStar used the opponent's observations to build a lower-variance value baseline during training ([Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). Using omniscient post-match data to *score* engagements offline never lets the bot see through fog during play. Using it as a runtime *input* would breach the fog-honesty ruling. The recommended line is that the bot's decision inputs and the network inputs come only from the fog-honest view, while offline scores may use the full record. Because this touches an existing ruling, the maintainer should confirm it explicitly.

## Score each engagement against its own prediction

Raw kill counts and raw harvesting are the known trap. SC2LE agents rewarded by the Blizzard score "converged to trivial strategies that avoid distracting workers from mining … simply preserving the initial mining process". That score is "not zero-sum since it is player-centric" ([Vinyals et al., arXiv 1708.04782](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.04782)). The robust recipe from OpenAI Five keeps win/loss as the true objective, makes every shaped term zero-sum by subtracting the enemy's, and values buildings with **two-thirds of the reward earned linearly as HP is lost and one-third as a lump sum on death** ([arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06680)). Potential-based shaping, F = γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged ([Wiewiora](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu//~ewiewior/03potential.pdf)).

For Cameo the **engagement score** should have three parts, normalised by the total value engaged so that skirmishes and decisive battles are comparable. **(i) Value traded:** enemy cost destroyed minus own cost lost, in resources, with structures on the 2/3-by-HP plus 1/3-on-death split, and harvesters and production priced at cost. **(ii) Performance against expectation:** the actual remainder (λ·A_f^o − β·B_f^o) minus the evaluator's predicted remainder at t₀. This credits *play quality* rather than army size. A bot that wins a fight it was predicted to win easily earns little; one that trades evenly from a predicted loss earns a lot. **(iii) Objective terms:** defended structures saved, expansions denied, defences cleared, and siege units preserved. The second part is what turns logging into learning. Because Stanescu's remainder is defined even when neither side is wiped out, training can use combat results "even if no side is dead" ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)). The **match score** used by SPSA and BO is the win/loss bit plus a small multiple of the zero-sum value margin. It must be validated by checking that the margin actually correlates with winning on Cameo's data before its weight is raised.

Credit assignment then works at three levels. Between matches, discounted bandits per decision family reward win/loss plus a small engagement term. This is the MegaBot and Steamhammer design ([Čertický et al.](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12961/12961-52-16478-1-2-20201228.pdf); [Steamhammer opponent model](http://satirist.org/ai/starcraft/blog/archives/474-the-opponent-model-in-Steamhammer-1.4.html)). Within a match, each decision record is credited with the change in the evaluator's value over its outcome window, the advantage V(after) − V(before) that OpenAI Five's dense reward was designed to make tractable ([arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06680)). For counterfactuals, the Lanchester predictor answers "what if this squad had held?" cheaply. It plays the role COMA's counterfactual baseline plays for multi-agent credit ([Foerster et al., arXiv 1705.08926](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08926)).

### The tactical questions become comparisons inside the same score

The specific behaviours in the request (army positioning relative to the enemy, response to base attacks, assault vs flank vs artillery-first siege) do not need separate reward functions. They are *actions* or *contexts* scored by the same engagement score. Attacking into defences is the network's or the gate's choice among direct assault, flank and siege-first, given a context that includes static-defence strength and the range advantage of available artillery. The score then says which choice traded best in that context. Stanescu's advice against bunker-heavy opponents, "the only way is to destroy the bunkers early enough", is exactly the situation where a siege-first policy should win the comparison ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)).

A "suicide into defences" metric falls out of the records: the evaluator predicted a loss including defence strength, the bot attacked anyway, and the value-traded ratio fell below a threshold. Those engagements should be near zero once the gate exists. Positioning is scored through the approach-geometry fields. A concave maximises the fraction of the army in range at first shot but is "the weakest to flanking" ([Liquipedia: Unit Positioning](https://liquipedia.net/starcraft2/Unit_Positioning)), so a learned engagement policy has to see both the bot's own spread and the enemy's. Base defence is scored by response latency and by value lost before response, with the detection stamp separating "didn't notice" from "noticed but was slow". These operational definitions are proposals: the research found no peer-reviewed metrics for flanking, siege order or concave quality. Their thresholds should be calibrated against replays the maintainer judges by eye.

## Variety proposes, the veto disposes

The perception research tells Cameo where to spend its variety budget. In a controlled study with 22 AI opponents, **83% of players could not recognise an AI that was literally a random number generator**. Players could not detect strategy switching or secret handicapping. Fun correlated with nothing measured: **r = 0.04 with perceived difficulty and −0.19 with actual difficulty**. What players did notice were shocking, memorable errors, and an AI that "switched targets too frequently was seen as 'computery'" ([Wetzel & Anderson, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 4](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter04_Player_Perception_of_AI_Opponents.pdf)). Invisible micro-jitter is therefore wasted. Visible macro variety (where the attack comes from, which unit mix appears, when the first push lands) gets noticed. A single visible blunder costs more than a dozen clever plays earn. Left 4 Dead's Director shows the cheap version of surprise: varying *where and when* pressure arrives meant experienced teams "were no longer able to expect where mobs would hit" ([Booth, Valve 2009](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)).

The design has three randomising layers and two safety layers. **Strategy layer:** a quality-diversity archive per faction on two player-visible axes, for example first-attack timing × army-vs-economy spend. Each cell keeps the best known knob vector of its kind. In Hearthstone, CMA-ME's archive produced a best policy at **65.9% win rate vs 45.2% for MAP-Elites and 44.8% for CMA-ES**, while filling more cells ([Fontaine et al., arXiv 1912.02400](https://arxiv.org/html/1912.02400v2)). That run used 500 CPUs and 200 games per evaluation. Cameo's version must fill a coarse grid (say 5×5) from ordinary match results instead of an offline search. The bot Thompson-samples a cell. Thompson sampling randomises naturally, tolerates outcomes that arrive only at game end, and stays unpredictable where UCB would be deterministic ([Chapelle & Li, NeurIPS 2011](http://papers.neurips.cc/paper/4321-an-empirical-evaluation-of-thompson-sampling.pdf)). **Pressure layer:** a Director-style draw of attack lane, harass target and timing window inside the chosen plan. **Knob layer:** jitter only within the range the data covers, snapping back to the baseline outside it, as SPIBB does by falling back to the baseline wherever data is thin ([Laroche et al., ICML 2019](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/laroche19a/laroche19a.pdf)).

The first safety layer is a **conservative floor on arms**. An archive cell or opening is eligible only if its lower confidence bound against the current enemy faction clears the hand-tuned default's posterior mean minus a margin. If nothing clears, the default plays. This is the arm-filter form of conservative bandits, which explore only while performance stays above (1−α) of the baseline ([Wu et al., ICML 2016](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v48/wu16.html)). The second is a **deterministic action veto**: no attack where the fog-honest evaluator predicts a loss including static-defence strength; no siege-free assault into defence density above a threshold; a home-defence reserve when an enemy attack is predicted. Retreat is gated too. Steamhammer retreats only if a 2-second retreat simulation says the retreat will succeed, because a doomed retreat is its own blunder ([satirist.org: movement simulation](http://satirist.org/ai/starcraft/blog/archives/1069-movement-simulation.html)). A **commitment rule** (hold a plan until a clear trigger fires) prevents the flip-flopping players read as "computery". Because the vetoes act *after* sampling, they cap the downside of every random draw. Counting vetoes per personality gives a free diagnostic: a personality that is constantly vetoed is mistuned.

Every randomised dimension should be regression-tested on its own. Beyond All Reason's bot, given a "Random" faction, stopped building extractors outside its spawn and played "as if it is just a very simple version of the AI" ([BYAR-Chobby #1024](https://github.com/beyond-all-reason/BYAR-Chobby/issues/1024)). A randomisation path that silently skipped initialisation looked like variety and was really a bug.

## Factions share a prior, and rebalances reset only the residuals

Every shipped multi-faction system uses one shared engine with per-faction *data*. Steamhammer keys weighted opening mixes by matchup, with "U" for an unknown opponent ([Steamhammer configuration](http://satirist.org/ai/starcraft/steamhammer/2.4.2/configuration.html)). AoE2 scripts use civ-conditional blocks. C&C Generals uses per-faction build lists and script teams. The AoE2 Barbarian AI's inability to play civilisations added later is the cold-start failure of purely hand-written per-faction data ([aoezone](https://aoezone.net/threads/barbarian-ai-on-de.162352/)). For ~25 factions and 625 ordered matchups, Cameo should do the same and put learning in a **hierarchy**: global → game family (TD/RA/TS/Generals/…) → faction → matchup. Each level shrinks toward its parent in proportion to its noise. Hierarchical Thompson sampling provably lowers regret as the shared prior becomes more informative ([Hong et al., AISTATS 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v151/hong22c.html)). The classic Efron–Morris shrinkage cut prediction error to **under one third** with 45 observations per group ([U. Akron summary](https://ideaexchange.uakron.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1722&context=honors_research_projects)), the same order as Cameo's per-matchup cell sizes. Contextual bandits that share features across cells beat context-free ones by more as data gets scarcer ([Li et al., WWW 2010](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1772690.1772758)). A new ContentPack faction then starts from its family's and role-neighbours' posteriors instead of from zero.

Reacting to the *enemy* faction needs no per-human storage. Steamhammer's tiered rule fits: a counter-mix if the enemy's typical plan is predictable, otherwise a random opening, with a discount factor that forgets old games and a re-prediction when the enemy race is revealed. Its author's verdict was "when it helps it helps a lot" ([satirist.org](http://satirist.org/ai/starcraft/blog/archives/474-the-opponent-model-in-Steamhammer-1.4.html)). Keyed by (enemy faction, map) instead of opponent name, plus in-match recognition of coarse plan classes, it delivers matchup-specific play and stays clean on privacy. One project constraint bites here: while A/B testing is limited to mirror matches until the rebalance, cross-faction posteriors receive only exploratory data. The cross-matchup layer can be built now but will not have earned trust until the rebalance lifts that limit.

Rebalance robustness follows from what is learned. The SC2 networks survived balancing because "the neural network is not looking at specific units, only their statistics", and kept working "as long as there weren't any radical changes in the game's mechanics" ([Game AI Pro ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter30_Using_Neural_Networks_to_Control_Agent_Threat_Response.pdf)). In contrast, a Honor of Kings policy keyed to one hero collapsed when "the change of target heroes differs the action meaning" ([Wei et al., HoK Arena](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.08483)). Cameo should therefore let every `apply_balance` run regenerate a bot-facing derived table: effective DPS per armour class from resolved `Versus`, cost-normalised LTD2, range and speed buckets, and the counter matrix. It should store learned values only as **bounded residuals** on that table, regularised toward 1.0. The prior then moves for free and only the residuals need re-adaptation. No published system auto-regenerates a bot's priors from a balance pipeline, and "fingerprint" invalidation (discounting a residual when the stats it was fitted on change) is a proposal, not established practice. Both are straightforward extensions of the Stanescu regulariser.

For the bandits, a rebalance is a *known* change point. Soft-resetting posteriors at the patch (for example multiplying Beta pseudo-counts by 0.2) beats waiting for drift detection, since discounted and sliding-window methods only match the √(ΥT) lower bound up to log factors ([Garivier & Moulines](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/0805.3415)). Detectors such as ADWIN, which carries false-positive and false-negative guarantees ([Bifet & Gavaldà](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220907178_Learning_from_Time-Changing_Data_with_Adaptive_Windowing)), still earn a place for *unannounced* drift, such as another agent's AI merge. Dev-build training can borrow OpenAI Five's domain randomisation, which perturbed hero armour, speed and regeneration explicitly to parallel sim-to-real transfer ([arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06680)). Perturbing stats by ±10–25% via the pipeline tunes knobs and the network to a *distribution* around the current balance. OpenAI Five's Rerun result also says when to stop adapting: once the rebalance freezes, a clean retrain of the network on the final ruleset is cheap (hours of CPU) and better than the continually patched version.

## What not to do

| Don't | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|
| Train a whole-game deep-RL policy | 60k games to beat Easy even with scripted macros ([arXiv 1809.07193](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.07193)); exceeds a year of budget for one matchup |
| Learn per-actor-id weights | Breaks on rebalance and on new factions ([HoK Arena](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.08483)); violates "never enumerate ids" |
| Reward raw kills, raw harvesting, or a player-centric score | Produced "just keep mining" degeneracy ([arXiv 1708.04782](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04782)) |
| SPSA, CMA-ES or BO over raw parameters | Hundreds of thousands of games ([nevergrad4sf](https://github.com/vondele/nevergrad4sf): "O(1M) games"); GP-BO fails past ~20 dimensions |
| Independent bandits per matchup × map cell | 625 matchups × arms leaves cells with almost no data; pool hierarchically |
| Randomise without a post-sampling veto, or switch plans freely | Shocking blunders and target flip-flopping are what players remember ([Wetzel & Anderson](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter04_Player_Perception_of_AI_Opponents.pdf)) |
| Ship a network without logged inputs/outputs, a scripted fallback and a switch | Black box; one fitness bug made the SC2 AI flee every ACU ([Game AI Pro ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter30_Using_Neural_Networks_to_Control_Agent_Threat_Response.pdf)) |
| Change a network's inputs, outputs or score without planning a full retrain | Any such change forces training from scratch (same source) |
| Feed omniscient state into runtime decisions | Breaches the fog-honesty ruling; omniscience is acceptable at most for offline scoring, pending a ruling |
| Put an LLM in the runtime loop | STRATA on OpenRA works as research ([arXiv 2609.38881](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.38881v1)) but adds an external model, latency and non-determinism to a host-only bot |
| Train continually through every patch and treat the result as final | The clean Rerun beat the patched OpenAI Five 98%+ for 20% of the compute ([arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06680)) |
| Test randomised paths only in aggregate | BAR's "Random" faction path silently crippled its bot ([BYAR-Chobby #1024](https://github.com/beyond-all-reason/BYAR-Chobby/issues/1024)) |

## Conclusion

The question "how many parameters can we learn?" has no single answer. It splits into "how many independent samples does each parameter get?", and Cameo's answer is tiered. Thousands of measured coefficients, tens of thousands of network weights over per-engagement decisions, hundreds of pooled bandit posteriors, and fewer than 25 win-rate-tuned knobs at any time make a bot that is genuinely data-driven without pretending a single PC is a TPU league. The order of work matters more than the method. The Lanchester-style evaluator and the engagement record come first, because the veto layer, the engagement score, the counterfactual credit and the network's training targets all depend on them. Its prediction-vs-outcome calibration is also the first honest measure of whether the bot "understands" a fight.

The more novel implication is that Cameo's balance pipeline and its bot-learning stack are the same machine viewed from two sides. The pipeline already computes the stat-derived quantities a rebalance-robust bot should treat as its prior, and the increment A/B harness is already the paired-match experiment SPSA and the bandits need. Wiring the pipeline to emit the bot's prior table makes every balance change a free update to the bot rather than a threat to it. Learning then shrinks to the residual the stats cannot explain, which is exactly the part worth spending matches on.
